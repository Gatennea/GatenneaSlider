# Irreversibility and Reachability in a Seam-Sliding Polyomino Puzzle

> **Status**: first draft, 2026-10-16. Markdown draft; to be converted to LaTeX (`amsart`) for arXiv.
> Written for arXiv (math.CO / cs.DM). All stated computational results are reproducible; see §9.
> Only *verified* data are cited: the permutation group order for (4,4,2) is **331,776**
> (NOT the single-seed artifact 165,888), and the full sticky-type verification is quoted for
> the two sizes where `-fullsticky` output exists.

---

## Abstract

We study a sliding puzzle on the square grid in which a move selects a horizontal or vertical
seam, takes a connected component on one side, and translates it by a fixed step $s$ parallel
to the seam, subject to non-overlap and connectedness of the result. The solved state is the
$m\times n$ rectangle.

The move set is asymmetric: a substantial fraction of moves (20.6% at $s=2$, 14.0% at $s=3$ on
the $4\times4$ board) have no legal inverse. We prove that this irreversibility has exactly one
mechanism: the translated component, in its new position, is no longer separable — it is not a
whole side-component of any coaxial seam, i.e. it has become "glued" to the rest. The proof is
combinatorial and independent of the board parameters $(m,n,s)$.

Two natural conjectures are refuted by exhaustive enumeration: the forward-reachable set and
the backward-reachable set are **in general incomparable** (they cross), and the coloring
invariant — the counts of tiles in each residue class mod $s$, which is conserved by every move
— is strictly weaker than solvability. We give a complete census for the $4\times4$ boards at
$s=2$ and $s=3$ (state counts, God's numbers, dead ends), show that on a family of sizes the
reachable permutation group on labelled tiles is the full symmetric group on each color class
(so any two same-color tiles can be exchanged, in contrast to the classical 15-puzzle where a
parity obstruction forbids this). We refute the natural conjecture that a state is unsolvable
exactly when it eventually flows into a dead end: every solvable state can be led into a dead end,
while most unsolvable states are trapped in isolated closed components and never reach one.
We also provide the exact distance distributions.

## 1. Introduction

### 1.1 The puzzle

Fix integers $m,n\ge 2$ and a step $s\ge 1$; put $K=mn$. A **configuration** is a
$4$-connected set of $K$ cells of the square grid $\mathbb Z^2$ (a polyomino of size $K$).
The **solved** configuration is the $m\times n$ rectangle.

**Move.** Let $L$ be a horizontal or vertical grid line meeting the bounding box of the current
configuration. The cells of the configuration on each side of $L$ fall into connected
components. A move selects one such component $C$ on one side and translates it by $s$ cells in
a direction **parallel** to $L$ (a horizontal seam admits only horizontal translations, a vertical
seam only vertical ones). The move is legal if, at every intermediate step of the translation,
$C$ does not overlap the cells on the other side, and if the union after translation is still
$4$-connected. Both sides must be non-empty.

The classical 15-puzzle is the case where one translates a single cell into an adjacent empty
square. Here one translates *whole connected components*, by a step $s$, and there is no distinguished
empty square: the gap is created by the translation itself. The connectivity requirement is an
essential additional constraint absent from the classical puzzle.

### 1.2 Motivation and relation to prior work

The classical puzzle has a well-developed theory. Johnson and Story (1879) introduced the parity
invariant that partitions labelled states into two inaccessible classes; Wilson (1974) classified
the reachable permutation group of the one-tile sliding puzzle on an arbitrary $2$-connected
graph $G$: the group contains $A_n$, equals $S_n$ exactly when $G$ is non-bipartite, and equals
$A_n$ for bipartite $G$, with two exceptional families (cycles of length $\ge 5$ and the graph
$\theta_0$). Hearn and Demaine (2005) and Demaine and Rudoy (2018) established hardness results.

Our move set is different, and we do **not** claim to generalize Wilson's theorem. Our
labelled-tile question — *when is the reachable permutation group the full symmetric group on
each residue class?* — is the **analogue** of Wilson's classification for this mechanic, and our
answer (full symmetric group on each class, for a family of sizes) exhibits a genuine contrast:
whereas the classical puzzle on a bipartite board is confined to even permutations, our "tight"
sizes admit arbitrary permutations within each color class, i.e. **no parity obstruction**.

We are not aware of prior study of this exact mechanic. Neighboring models that slide whole
rows/columns, or rotate pieces on a torus, or move single tiles, do not include the operation
"cut along a seam, take one side's connected component, translate by $s$, stay connected."

### 1.3 Results

1. **Sticky-type uniqueness (§4).** A move with no legal inverse is exactly a move whose
   translated component has lost separability. Proved for all $(m,n,s)$; verified exhaustively
   (edge by edge) on $4\times4$, $s=2$ (880,858/880,858) and $s=3$ (12,926/12,926).
2. **Refutation of two natural conjectures (§3).** The forward- and backward-reachable sets
   cross; the coloring invariant is strictly weaker than solvability.
3. **Census (§5).** Complete enumeration for $(4,4,2)$ and $(4,4,3)$, including God's numbers
   17 and 29, dead ends 9 and 6.
4. **Permutation group (§6).** For a family of sizes the reachable group is the full symmetric
   group on each color class; hence any two same-color tiles can be exchanged.
5. **The dead-end basin (§5.1).** The conjecture "unsolvable $\iff$ flows into a dead end" is
   **false**: all solvable states can reach a dead end, yet most unsolvable states (319/328,
   1435/1442) never can — they are trapped in isolated closed components.
6. **Distance distributions (§7).** Exact distributions for five sizes (OEIS-style).

## 2. Formal model and notation

Let $\Omega$ be the set of configurations. Write $x\to y$ if a legal move takes $x$ to $y$.
Directed graph $\mathcal G$ on $\Omega$.

- **Coloring invariant.** Assign to a cell at $(x,y)$ the class $(x \bmod s,\, y \bmod s)\in
  (\mathbb Z/s)^2$. A move translates by $s$ cells, so **every tile keeps its class**. Hence the
  vector of class-sizes (the *color-weight vector*) is invariant. For $s=1$ there is one class
  and the invariant is vacuous.
- **$A$** (coloring set): all configurations with the same color-weight vector as the solved state.
  Then $\mathrm{cone}\subseteq A$ and $B\subseteq A$.
- **$\mathrm{cone}(g)$**: configurations reachable from the solved state $g$.
- **$B$**: configurations from which $g$ is reachable.
- **Solvable**: $\mathrm{cone}\cap B$.
- **Dead end**: $\mathrm{cone}\setminus B$. **External predecessor**: $B\setminus\mathrm{cone}$.
  **Incomparable**: $A\setminus(\mathrm{cone}\cup B)$.
- **God's number**: $\max_{x\in B} d(x,g)$ where $d$ is directed distance ("return metric").
  The forward metric ($\max_{x\in\mathrm{cone}} d(g,x)$) is different in general.

---

## 3. The coloring invariant is not sufficient; the two reachable sets cross

**Theorem 3.1 (conservation).** Every legal move preserves the color-weight vector; consequently
$\mathrm{cone}\cup B\subseteq A$.

**Refutation 1.** $\mathrm{cone}=B$ is false. On $(4,4,2)$: $9$ dead ends ($\mathrm{cone}\setminus B\neq\emptyset$)
and $1{,}227$ external predecessors ($B\setminus\mathrm{cone}\neq\emptyset$); on $(4,4,3)$, $6$ and
$1{,}408$. Across $17$ enumerated sizes, $8$ have $\mathrm{cone}\subsetneq B$, $7$ have equality,
and the two $4\times4$ sizes are incomparable. Since a single counterexample refutes a universal
claim, the conjecture "reachable implies returnable" is false in general.

**Refutation 2.** $A=B$ is false. Across all $17$ enumerated sizes $|A\setminus B|>0$ (e.g.
$328$ for $(4,4,2)$, $1{,}442$ for $(4,4,3)$; the minimum is $1$).

Hence the coloring invariant — computable in linear time and the natural analogue of the
Johnson–Story parity invariant — is a **necessary but not sufficient** solvability criterion.
The two sets $\mathrm{cone}$ and $B$ are **incomparable**, not nested.

## 4. The unique mechanism of one-step irreversibility

This is our main theorem. Let $u\to v$ be a legal move translating component $C$ by $s$ cells
parallel to the seam, and let $C'$ be the image of $C$ in $v$.

Say $C'$ is **selectable** in $v$ if there is a seam coaxial with the translation direction
having $C'$ as a whole side-component.

**Lemma 4.1 (selectable $\Rightarrow$ reversible).** If $C'$ is selectable in $v$, then the
reverse translation of $C'$ by $s$ cells is legal at every intermediate step, and its endpoint
is exactly $u$.

*Proof.* Write $v=(u\setminus C)\cup C'$ with $C'=C+s\mathbf d$ and $(u\setminus C)\cap C'=\emptyset$,
so $v\setminus C'=u\setminus C$. At intermediate step $i$ the configuration is
$(u\setminus C)\cup(C+(s-i)\mathbf d)$; for $i=s$ this is $u$ (connected), and for $i<s$ it is an
intermediate configuration of the forward move (which was connected and non-overlapping by the
legality of $u\to v$). Direction and seam are coaxial by hypothesis. $\square$

**Theorem 4.2 (sticky-type uniqueness).** If there is no legal move $v\to u$ — i.e. the move
$u\to v$ is not reversible in one step — then $C'$ is **not** selectable in $v$.

*Proof.* Immediate contrapositive of Lemma 4.1. $\square$

**Interpretation.** Irreversibility can only manifest as *loss of separability*: the translated
component has become glued to the rest, so that no coaxial seam isolates it. The competing
mechanism — "the component is still selectable but moving it back collides or disconnects" —
is logically impossible under these rules.

**Corollary 4.3.** In any classification of one-step moves into (i) reversible, (ii) sticky
(not selectable), (iii) selectable-but-not-returnable, (iv) anomalous, classes (iii) and (iv)
are empty.

The theorem is **one-way**: not selectable does not in general imply irreversibility
(self-overlapping translations, where $C\cap C'\neq\emptyset$, can remain reversible via a
different seam).

**Exhaustive verification.** Every edge of $\mathcal G$ with both endpoints in $B$ was checked:

| size | one-step irreversible edges | sticky (not selectable) | other mechanisms |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| $(4,4,2)$ | 880,858 | 880,858 (100%) | 0 |
| $(4,4,3)$ | 12,926 | 12,926 (100%) | 0 |

The one-step irreversible fraction is 20.63% of $B$-internal edges for $(4,4,2)$ and 13.99% for
$(4,4,3)$.

**The same exhaustive check was carried out on all $17$ enumerated sizes ($K\le 16$); in every
case the sticky count equals the total number of one-step irreversible edges — i.e. $100\%$ —
with zero reversal-violation and zero anomalous edges.** (Sizes $(5,3,2)$ and $(3,5,2)$ contribute
$249{,}113$ irreversible edges each, all sticky; the remaining sizes are recorded in the sweep
summary table.)

*Remark.* An earlier sampling-based version of this check was invalidated by two bugs
in the checker (a wrong set complement in computing the image; and a missing coaxial restriction
in the selectability test). Only the post-fix exhaustive results are quoted here.

## 5. Census of the $4\times4$ boards

| quantity | $(4,4,2)$ | $(4,4,3)$ |
| --- | ---: | ---: |
| $\|A\|$ (coloring set) | 559,832 | 24,605 |
| $\|\mathrm{cone}\|$ | 558,286 | 21,761 |
| $\|B\|$ | 559,504 | 23,163 |
| $\|\mathrm{cone}\cap B\|$ (solvable) | 558,277 | 21,755 |
| $\|\mathrm{cone}\cup B\|$ | 559,513 | 23,169 |
| dead ends | 9 | 6 |
| external predecessors | 1,227 | 1,408 |
| incomparable | 319 | 1,436 |
| God's number (return metric) | 17 | 29 |
| one-step irreversible ($B$-internal) | 880,858 (20.63%) | 12,926 (13.99%) |
| permutation group order | 331,776 | 384 |

For $(4,4,2)$ the forward metric gives $23$, not $17$; the discrepancy is itself a consequence of
the abundance of one-way edges.

**Dead-end structure.** For all $15$ dead ends across both sizes: (a) some row or some column
fails to contain an $s$-cell contiguous run (necessary condition, 15/15); (b) no $2\times2$ hole
(15/15); (c) the "strong" condition (all rows *and* all columns fail) holds 0/15 — it is
vacuously true but useless. Bounding-box short side $\le 3$ holds 9/9 at $s=2$ but 0/6 at $s=3$,
so it is not a general feature.

### 5.1 The dead-end basin refutes the natural conjecture

Define the **basin** of the dead ends as $\mathrm{Basin}=\{x : x \text{ can reach some dead end}\}$.
It is computed by a single reverse BFS from the dead-end set along reverse edges.

A very natural conjecture is: *a state is unsolvable if and only if it eventually flows into a
dead end*. If true, the dead ends would be the entire source of unsolvability. **The conjecture
is false**, and it fails in an instructive way. A state $x$ reaches a dead end iff the reverse
graph carries a path from a dead end to $x$, giving:

| | $(4,4,2)$ | $(4,4,3)$ |
| --- | ---: | ---: |
| $\lvert\mathrm{Basin}\rvert$ | 559,513 | 23,170 |
| $\lvert\mathrm{Basin}\cap B\rvert$ (solvable states that can fall into a dead end) | **559,504 $=\lvert B\rvert$ (all)** | **23,163 $=\lvert B\rvert$ (all)** |
| unsolvable $\lvert A-B\rvert$ | 328 | 1,442 |
| unsolvable states that **cannot** reach a dead end | **319** | **1,435** |

Two conclusions, identical for both sizes:

1. **Every solvable state can, if it plays badly, walk into a dead end.** So "can reach a dead
   end" does not imply unsolvability.
2. **Most unsolvable states can never reach any dead end** (319 of 328; 1435 of 1442). They are
   trapped in isolated weak components of the coloring set that contain no dead end and cannot
   reach one.

Hence the dead ends are *not* the root of unsolvability. Unsolvability is dominated by
**isolated dead components** (states weakly connected to, but not reachable from, the goal
component), not by "flowing into a dead end". For $(4,4,2)$ the decomposition is exact: the 328
unsolvable states are the 9 dead ends themselves (which trivially lie in their own basin) plus
319 states sitting in the 76 isolated dead components; and
$\mathrm{Basin}=B\cup\{\text{9 dead ends}\}$, of size $559{,}513=\lvert\mathrm{cone}\cup B\rvert$.

## 6. Labelled tiles: the reachable permutation group

Label the $K$ tiles. A closed walk from the solved state back to the solved state induces a
permutation of the labels; the set of such permutations is a group $\Gamma$.

**Upper bound.** Tiles never leave their color class, so
$\Gamma\subseteq\prod_{c}(\text{symmetric group on class } c)$, giving
$|\Gamma|\le\prod_c |c|!$.

**Method.** We collect generators by random walks on $\mathcal G$ and take the multiplicative
closure of the generated subgroup. Since closure $\subseteq\Gamma\subseteq$ upper bound, hitting
the bound proves equality. *(This is a deterministic conclusion once the bound is attained, not a
statistical estimate — but the generators must be genuinely collected: see the caveat below.)*

| size | upper bound | attained $|\Gamma|$ | any two same-color tiles exchangeable? |
| --- | ---: | ---: | --- |
| $(2,2,2)$ | 1 | 1 | trivially |
| $(3,3,2)$ | 96 | 96 | yes |
| $(4,3,2)$, $(3,4,2)$ | 2,304 | 2,304 | yes |
| $(5,3,2)$, $(3,5,2)$ | 207,360 | 207,360 | yes |
| $(3,3,1)$ | 362,880 | 362,880 | yes |
| $(4,4,2)$ | 331,776 | **331,776** | yes |
| $(4,4,3)$ | 384 | 384 | yes |
| $(6,2,2)$, $(2,6,2)$ | 1,296 | **1** | **no** |

Since the symmetric group is generated by transpositions, attaining the bound means every
transposition of two same-color tiles is realizable: there is a sequence of moves, from the
solved state back to the solved state, whose net effect is to exchange two chosen same-color
tiles and fix all others. This is a statement about the *net effect of a sequence*, not about a
single move.

**Contrast with the classical puzzle.** On a bipartite board the classical 15-puzzle reaches only
even permutations. Here, at the sizes where the bound is attained, there is **no parity
obstruction**: the full symmetric group on each color class is reached.

**Caveats (methodological, and necessary for reproducibility).**
1. A single-seed random walk of 300k steps on $(4,4,2)$ stalled at subgroup order 165,888, exactly
   half the true value, i.e. trapped in an index-$2$ subgroup. The value 331,776 comes from longer
   pure random walks with multiple seeds. **165,888 must not be quoted as the group order.**
2. Strongly biased walks on $(4,4,3)$ yielded only 192 (an undersampling artifact); unbiased walks
   give 384.
3. Several sizes have $|\Gamma|=1$: every closed walk induces the identity, so no tile can be
   permuted at all, despite thousands of configurations.

**Open (construction).** Attaining the bound proves *existence* of exchange sequences. Explicit
short sequences (a table of transposition formulae) are **not** constructed here. A promising route
for $s=2$ is to coarsen the board by $2\times2$ blocks so that an $s=2$ move becomes an $s=1$ move
on the half-size board; this requires proving that the "$2$-aligned sub-puzzle is isomorphic to the
$s=1$ half-size puzzle", which we have **not** proved. For $s\ge 3$ one must search the Cayley graph.

These are finite sequences ($0\le k\le$ God's number), in the same genre as OEIS A089473

## 8. Open problems

*(The dead-end-basin conjecture has been settled — and refuted — see §5.1.)*

1. **Isolated dead components.** Since most unsolvable states are trapped in closed components of
   the coloring set rather than flowing into dead ends (§5.1), characterize those components:
   their sizes (up to 49 and 36 states observed), and a combinatorial criterion for membership.
   This is now the primary open problem, replacing the basin conjecture.
2. **Sufficiency for dead ends.** Is "some row or column lacks an $s$-run, and no $2\times2$ hole"
   sufficient? (Necessity holds, 15/15.)
3. **Explicit transposition formulae**, and the minimal length of an exchange sequence.
4. **The $2$-aligned coarsening lemma** for $s=2$ (§6), and the $s\ge3$ case.
5. **God's number**, theoretical explanation of the return/forward discrepancy (17 vs 23).
6. **Larger boards** ($K>16$) and other geometries.

## 9. Conclusion and a note on other board shapes

We have analyzed a seam-sliding polyomino puzzle: we proved that one-step irreversibility has a
single mechanism (loss of separability) uniformly in the board parameters; refuted the two most
natural conjectures about solvability (the forward and backward reachable sets cross, and the
coloring invariant is too weak); refuted the conjecture that unsolvability is caused by dead ends;
gave a complete census of the $4\times4$ boards; identified the reachable permutation group on
labelled tiles; and tabulated the exact distance distributions.

The framework is, to a large extent, independent of the particular board shape. The
graph-theoretic part -- forward/backward reachability, the solvable set, and the permutation-group
analysis with upper bound $\prod_c |c|!$ -- applies verbatim to any puzzle whose moves are edges of
a state graph, and the proof of the irreversibility theorem uses only the fact that a move
translates a side-component along a seam and that the reverse translation retraces the forward
path. We therefore expect both to carry over unchanged to the triangular and ``rice-grid''
($\mathrm{mi}$) variants supported by the same game engine.

What does *not* transfer without genuinely new work is the explicit description of those variants.
Our formal model is tied to a rectangular board: the solved state is an $m\times n$ rectangle, the
seams are horizontal or vertical lines, and the conserved coloring is the coset structure of
$\mathbb Z^2/s\mathbb Z^2$. For a triangular or $\mathrm{mi}$ board, the legal seams, the solved
state, and above all the translation lattice generated by the legal moves are all different; the
$\mathrm{mi}$ board is moreover known to exhibit a "double lattice" at odd steps, a phenomenon we
have not yet explained. A faithful single description covering all three shapes would require a
more abstract framework -- one that treats a seam as a general separator of a region, and derives
the conserved coloring from the translation lattice generated by legal moves rather than from the
coordinates of a rectangular grid. We leave this as future work; the results above concern the
square board.

## 10. Reproducibility

All enumerations were produced by a C++ engine performing exhaustive forward and backward BFS
over the coloring set, plus a Python reference implementation; the two agree. Distance
distributions are derived directly from the backward-BFS tables. Code and outputs are in the
project repository (`experiments/math_repro/`); the relevant commands are documented in the
reproducibility report.
